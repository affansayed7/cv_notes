2Triangulation: the problem

Definition
Triangulation: given a set of (noisy) matched points \(\{\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}'_i\}\) and the camera matrices \(\mathbf{P}, \mathbf{P}'\), estimate the 3D point \(\mathbf{X}\).

Slide question: which 3D points map to x? Not just one: every point on a ray from the camera center through x projects to x. One image alone can't tell how far along the ray the point is (depth is lost).

3Backprojection: computing the ray

Slide question: how can you compute this ray? A line needs two points. Create two points on it and connect them:

  1. The camera center C. Every ray of the camera passes through it. (From Module 09: C is the null space of P, i.e. \(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{0}\).)
  2. \(\mathbf{P}^{+}\mathbf{x}\), where \(\mathbf{P}^{+} = \mathbf{P}^\top(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{P}^\top)^{-1}\) is the pseudo-inverse of the 3×4 matrix P.
Definition
This procedure (camera center + \(\mathbf{P}^+\mathbf{x}\), joined by a line) is called backprojection: turning a 2D image point back into a 3D ray.
Slide question: why does P⁺x map to x?
Project it: \(\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{P}^{+}\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{P}^\top(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{P}^\top)^{-1}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{I}\,\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}\). ✔ So \(\mathbf{P}^+\mathbf{x}\) is a 3D point that lands exactly on x, hence it lies on the ray.

4Find the 3D point: will the rays intersect?

Slide question: will the lines intersect?
In theory yes, at X. In practice almost never. Pixel positions are noisy (detection error, rounding, imperfect calibration), so the two 3D rays are skew lines: they pass close to each other but don't touch. No single solution exists due to noise, so we look for the 3D point that best fits both views. That's a least-squares problem.

5x = PX is a similarity relation

Slide question: can we compute X from a single correspondence x? In \(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{X}\), x and P are known, but one view only gives the ray, so no. We also can't just "solve" the equation directly, because:

Key point
\(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{X}\) is a similarity relation (equality up to scale), because it involves homogeneous coordinates. Written with a real equals sign:
\[ \mathbf{x} = \alpha\,\mathbf{P}\mathbf{X} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \begin{bmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{bmatrix} = \alpha\begin{bmatrix} p_1 & p_2 & p_3 & p_4\\ p_5 & p_6 & p_7 & p_8 \\ p_9 & p_{10} & p_{11} & p_{12}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}X\\Y\\Z\\1\end{bmatrix} \]
Same ray direction, but differs by an unknown scale factor α. (The slide labels this side "heterogeneous coordinate", meaning the actual vector values rather than the up-to-scale class.)

Slide question: how do we solve for unknowns in a similarity relation? Get rid of α. The trick is the cross product.

6Linear algebra reminder: cross & dot product

Cross product
Takes two vectors and returns a vector perpendicular to both:
\[ \mathbf{c} = \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} a_2b_3 - a_3b_2 \\ a_3b_1 - a_1b_3 \\ a_1b_2 - a_2b_1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{a} = 0,\quad \mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{b} = 0 \]
Remember this!!!
The cross product of two vectors in the same direction is the zero vector: \(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0}\), and also \(\mathbf{a}\times(\alpha\mathbf{a}) = \mathbf{0}\) for any scale α.

Cross product as a matrix multiplication (we'll need this for the Essential matrix):

\[ \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} = [\mathbf{a}]_\times\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -a_3 & a_2 \\ a_3 & 0 & -a_1 \\ -a_2 & a_1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}b_1\\b_2\\b_3\end{bmatrix} \]

\([\mathbf{a}]_\times\) is skew-symmetric: \([\mathbf{a}]_\times^\top = -[\mathbf{a}]_\times\) (zero diagonal, mirrored entries with flipped signs).

Compare with: dot product
The dot product of two orthogonal vectors is (scalar) zero. Since c = a × b is orthogonal to both a and b: \(\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{b} = 0\).

7Back to triangulation: x × PX = 0

Slide question: how can we rewrite x = αPX using vector products? x and PX point in the same direction, so:

\[ \boxed{\;\mathbf{x}\times\mathbf{P}\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{0}\;} \qquad\text{(cross product of same-direction vectors is zero, and this equality removes the scale factor α)} \]

Expand. Write P by its rows \(\mathbf{p}_1^\top, \mathbf{p}_2^\top, \mathbf{p}_3^\top\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{bmatrix} = \alpha\begin{bmatrix} \text{—}\ \mathbf{p}_1^\top\ \text{—} \\ \text{—}\ \mathbf{p}_2^\top\ \text{—} \\ \text{—}\ \mathbf{p}_3^\top\ \text{—}\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{X} = \alpha\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{p}_1^\top\mathbf{X}\\ \mathbf{p}_2^\top\mathbf{X}\\ \mathbf{p}_3^\top\mathbf{X}\end{bmatrix} \]

Take the cross product (with the image point normalized so z = 1):

\[ \begin{bmatrix}x\\y\\1\end{bmatrix}\times\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{p}_1^\top\mathbf{X}\\ \mathbf{p}_2^\top\mathbf{X}\\ \mathbf{p}_3^\top\mathbf{X}\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} y\,\mathbf{p}_3^\top\mathbf{X} - \mathbf{p}_2^\top\mathbf{X} \\ \mathbf{p}_1^\top\mathbf{X} - x\,\mathbf{p}_3^\top\mathbf{X} \\ x\,\mathbf{p}_2^\top\mathbf{X} - y\,\mathbf{p}_1^\top\mathbf{X} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\0\end{bmatrix} \]
Only 2 equations, not 3
The third line is a linear combination of the first two: it equals −(x·row 1 + y·row 2). Check: \(-x(y\mathbf{p}_3^\top - \mathbf{p}_2^\top) - y(\mathbf{p}_1^\top - x\mathbf{p}_3^\top) = x\mathbf{p}_2^\top - y\mathbf{p}_1^\top\). ✔ (The slide says "x times the first line plus y times the second", which is the same thing up to sign.)
⇒ One 2D-to-3D point correspondence gives you 2 equations.

8Stacking both views: AX = 0

Remove the third row and rearrange as a system on the unknowns X:

\[ \begin{bmatrix} y\,\mathbf{p}_3^\top - \mathbf{p}_2^\top \\ \mathbf{p}_1^\top - x\,\mathbf{p}_3^\top \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbf{A}_i\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{0} \]

Now concatenate the 2D points from both images: two rows from camera one, two rows from camera two:

\[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} y\,\mathbf{p}_3^\top - \mathbf{p}_2^\top \\ \mathbf{p}_1^\top - x\,\mathbf{p}_3^\top \\ y'\,\mathbf{p}_3'^\top - \mathbf{p}_2'^\top \\ \mathbf{p}_1'^\top - x'\,\mathbf{p}_3'^\top \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\0\\0\end{bmatrix} \qquad \mathbf{A}\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{0} \]
Sanity check: dimensions?
Each \(\mathbf{p}_i^\top\) is 1×4, so A is 4×4. X is 4×1 (homogeneous 3D point [X, Y, Z, W]). The zero vector is 4×1.
4 equations, 3 real unknowns (X is up to scale), so the system is overdetermined. That's good, because with noise it gives a best fit.
How do we solve a homogeneous linear system? SVD!
X = the right singular vector of A with the smallest singular value (last column of V in A = UΣVᵀ). Then divide by its 4th entry to get (X, Y, Z). This method is called linear (DLT) triangulation. Full reasoning in Section 22.

9Epipolar geometry: vocabulary

TermDefinition
BaselineThe line joining the two camera centers o and o′.
Epipole (e, e′)Projection of the other camera's center on the image plane (e = image of o′ in camera 1). Equivalently, where the baseline pierces each image plane.
Epipolar planeThe plane containing the baseline and the 3D point p, i.e. the triangle o–p–o′.
Epipolar line (l, l′)Intersection of the epipolar plane with an image plane. It always passes through the epipole.
One family, many planes
Every 3D point gives its own epipolar plane, but all of them contain the baseline. They're like pages of a book rotating around its spine (the baseline). So all epipolar lines in an image pass through the same point, the epipole.

11The epipolar constraint

Another way to construct the epipolar plane, this time given x: backproject x to a ray in 3D. The plane through this ray and the baseline is the epipolar plane, and its intersection with image 2 is the epipolar line l′.

Epipolar constraint
Potential matches for x lie on the epipolar line l′. We don't know the depth of X, but wherever it is on the ray, its image in camera 2 is somewhere on l′.

13Why it matters: stereo matching

Task
The epipolar constraint is an important concept for stereo vision. Task: match a point in the left image to a point in the right image (the slides use a courtyard building photographed twice).
  • Slide question: how would you do it? Naively, compare the patch around x with every pixel in the right image, which is slow and causes many false matches.
  • We want to avoid search over the entire image.
  • The epipolar constraint reduces the search to a single line, l′: a 2D search becomes 1D.
  • Next question: how do you compute the epipolar line? → the Essential matrix.

14The Essential matrix: motivation

Definition
The Essential Matrix E is a 3×3 matrix that encodes epipolar geometry. Given a point in one image, multiplying by the essential matrix tells us the epipolar line in the second image:
\[ \boxed{\;\mathbf{E}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{l}'\;} \]

15Lines as vectors: xᵀl = 0

A 2D line \(ax + by + c = 0\) is written as a 3-vector \(\mathbf{l} = [a\ \ b\ \ c]^\top\) (homogeneous, so defined up to scale).

Slide question: if point x is on line l, then xᵀl = ?

\[ \mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{l} = \begin{bmatrix}x & y & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a\\b\\c\end{bmatrix} = ax + by + c = \mathbf{0} \]
Example
Line \(\mathbf{l} = [1, -1, 0]\) is \(x - y = 0\) (the diagonal). Point (3, 3): \(\ [3,3,1]\cdot[1,-1,0] = 3 - 3 + 0 = 0\) ✔ on the line. Point (3, 4): \(3 - 4 = -1 \ne 0\), not on the line.

16Putting it together: x′ᵀEx = 0

Slide question: if \(\mathbf{x}'^\top\mathbf{l}' = 0\) and \(\mathbf{E}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{l}'\), then \(\mathbf{x}'^\top\mathbf{E}\mathbf{x} = ?\) Substitute l′:

\[ \boxed{\;\mathbf{x}'^\top\mathbf{E}\,\mathbf{x} = 0\;} \]

x′ lies on the epipolar line of x, and that's exactly what this one scalar equation says.

18Where does the Essential matrix come from? (derivation)

Ingredient 1: rigid motion. Camera 2 is camera 1 moved by rotation R and translation t:

\[ \mathbf{x}' = \mathbf{R}(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{t}) \]

Slide question: does this look familiar? Yes: it's the camera-camera transform, just like the world-camera transform \(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_c = \mathbf{R}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_w - \tilde{\mathbf{C}})\) from Module 09.

Ingredient 2: coplanarity. The three vectors x, t, x′ are coplanar.

  • Slide question: \(\mathbf{x}^\top(\mathbf{t}\times\mathbf{x}) = ?\) 0. The cross product \(\mathbf{t}\times\mathbf{x}\) is a vector orthogonal to the plane, and the dot product of orthogonal vectors is 0.
  • Slide question: \((\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{t})^\top(\mathbf{t}\times\mathbf{x}) = ?\) Also 0: \(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{t}\) also lies in the plane (it's the direction from o′ to the point, written in camera-1 coordinates).
\[ (\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{t})^\top(\mathbf{t}\times\mathbf{x}) = 0 \]

Putting it together (step by step)

StepEquationWhat we did
1\((\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{t})^\top(\mathbf{t}\times\mathbf{x}) = 0\)coplanarity
2\((\mathbf{x}'^\top\mathbf{R})(\mathbf{t}\times\mathbf{x}) = 0\)From rigid motion, \(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{x}'\) (R is a rotation, so \(\mathbf{R}^{-1} = \mathbf{R}^\top\)). Transposing gives \(\mathbf{x}'^\top\mathbf{R}\).
3\((\mathbf{x}'^\top\mathbf{R})([\mathbf{t}]_\times\mathbf{x}) = 0\)Use the skew-symmetric matrix to represent the cross product
4\(\mathbf{x}'^\top(\mathbf{R}[\mathbf{t}]_\times)\mathbf{x} = 0\)Regroup (matrix multiplication is associative)
5\(\boxed{\mathbf{x}'^\top\mathbf{E}\,\mathbf{x} = 0},\quad \mathbf{E} = \mathbf{R}[\mathbf{t}]_\times\)Name it: the Essential Matrix [Longuet-Higgins 1981]
So E depends only on the camera motion
E is built from R and t only (the extrinsics between the two cameras). No K appears, because E works on camera coordinates. Since \([\mathbf{t}]_\times\) has rank 2, E has rank 2. It has 5 DOF: 3 for rotation + 2 for the translation direction, since the overall scale is lost.
(The rank and DOF facts are standard results from Hartley–Zisserman, not stated on the slides.)

Assumption (important!)
All of this holds for 2D points expressed in the camera coordinate system, i.e. the intrinsic matrices are identities (K = I). Real images are in pixels → next section.
Why Ee = 0?
Every epipolar line passes through e, so \(\mathbf{e}^\top\mathbf{l} = 0\) for every \(\mathbf{l} = \mathbf{E}^\top\mathbf{x}'\), i.e. \((\mathbf{E}\mathbf{e})^\top\mathbf{x}' = 0\) for all x′. That forces Ee = 0.

20The Fundamental matrix F

Slide question: how do you generalize to non-identity intrinsic matrices?

Definition
The fundamental matrix is a generalization of the essential matrix, where the assumption of identity intrinsic matrices is removed.

E operates on 2D coordinates expressed in the camera coordinate system (\(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\)). Convert pixels to camera coordinates with the inverse intrinsics:

\[ \underbrace{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}_{\text{camera point}} = \mathbf{K}^{-1}\underbrace{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{image point}}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{x}}' = \mathbf{K}'^{-1}\mathbf{x}' \]

Substitute into \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}'^\top\mathbf{E}\hat{\mathbf{x}} = 0\), writing out the epipolar constraint in terms of image coordinates:

\[ (\mathbf{K}'^{-1}\mathbf{x}')^\top\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{x}) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \mathbf{x}'^\top(\mathbf{K}'^{-\top}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{K}^{-1})\mathbf{x} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{\;\mathbf{x}'^\top\mathbf{F}\,\mathbf{x} = 0\;} \]

(One slide line shows "K′⁻ᵀEK⁻¹x = 0" without the leading x′ᵀ; that's a typo. The next line is the correct one.)

Same equation works in image coordinates!
\(\mathbf{x}'^\top\mathbf{F}\mathbf{x} = 0\). F maps pixels to epipolar lines: \(\mathbf{l}' = \mathbf{F}\mathbf{x}\). Every property of E carries over by swapping E → F (now with points in image coordinates):
  • \(\mathbf{x}'^\top\mathbf{F}\mathbf{x} = 0\)
  • \(\mathbf{l}' = \mathbf{F}\mathbf{x},\quad \mathbf{l} = \mathbf{F}^\top\mathbf{x}'\)
  • \(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0},\quad \mathbf{e}'^\top\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}\)

Breaking down the fundamental matrix

\[ \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{K}'^{-\top}\mathbf{E}\mathbf{K}^{-1} \qquad\qquad \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{K}'^{-\top}[\mathbf{t}_\times]\mathbf{R}\mathbf{K}^{-1} \]

F depends on both intrinsic (K, K′) and extrinsic (R, t) parameters.

Note on notation
The derivation gave \(\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{R}[\mathbf{t}]_\times\), but this slide writes \([\mathbf{t}]_\times\mathbf{R}\). Both forms appear in textbooks; they correspond to different conventions for how t is defined (which camera's frame it is measured in). For exams: E = R[t]ₓ from the slide's derivation, and F = K′⁻ᵀ E K⁻¹.

Slide question: how would you solve for F? From many matches \(\mathbf{x}'^\top_m\mathbf{F}\mathbf{x}_m = 0\) → the 8-point algorithm.

21The 8-point algorithm: setting up

Assume you have M matched image points \(\{\mathbf{x}_m, \mathbf{x}'_m\},\ m = 1,\dots,M\). Each correspondence should satisfy \(\mathbf{x}'^\top_m\mathbf{F}\mathbf{x}_m = 0\).

Slide question: how would you solve for the 3×3 F matrix? SVD! Set up a homogeneous linear system with 9 unknowns (f₁ … f₉).

\[ \begin{bmatrix} x'_m & y'_m & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} f_1 & f_2 & f_3 \\ f_4 & f_5 & f_6 \\ f_7 & f_8 & f_9 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x_m \\ y_m \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = 0 \]

Slide question: how many equations do you get from one correspondence? ONE correspondence gives you ONE equation (the result is a scalar). Multiplying out:

\[ x'_m x_m f_1 + x'_m y_m f_2 + x'_m f_3 + y'_m x_m f_4 + y'_m y_m f_5 + y'_m f_6 + x_m f_7 + y_m f_8 + f_9 = 0 \]
Note: the slide's row ordering
The slide writes the row as \([x_m x'_m,\ x_m y'_m,\ x_m,\ y_m x'_m,\ y_m y'_m,\ y_m,\ x'_m,\ y'_m,\ 1]\). That's the expansion above with the roles of (x, y) and (x′, y′) swapped, so the 9-vector it solves for is really Fᵀ read row-wise. Both work as long as you reshape the solution consistently. The version above matches \(\mathbf{x}'^\top\mathbf{F}\mathbf{x}\) directly.

Stack M correspondences into an M×9 system:

\[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} x'_1x_1 & x'_1y_1 & x'_1 & y'_1x_1 & y'_1y_1 & y'_1 & x_1 & y_1 & 1 \\ \vdots & & & & \vdots & & & & \vdots \\ x'_Mx_M & x'_My_M & x'_M & y'_Mx_M & y'_My_M & y'_M & x_M & y_M & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}\ (M\times 9)}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}f_1\\ \vdots \\ f_9\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{f}} = \mathbf{0} \]

Slide question: how many equations do you need?

Hence, the 8-point algorithm!
9 unknowns, but F is only defined up to scale (F and 2F give the same constraint), so 8 independent unknowns. Each point pair contributes only one scalar equation, so we need at least 8 points.

Note: this is different from homography estimation, where each point pair contributes 2 equations (H: 8 DOF / 2 = 4 points).

22Solving a homogeneous linear system: total least squares & SVD

Slide question: how do you solve AX = 0? X = 0 is always a solution, but it's useless. Fix the scale instead:

Total Least Squares
\[ \text{minimize}\ \ \|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\|^2 \qquad \text{subject to}\ \ \|\mathbf{x}\|^2 = 1 \]
Solution: SVD! Write \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{U}\Sigma\mathbf{V}^\top\). The minimizer is the column of V corresponding to the smallest singular value (the last column of V, i.e. the last row of Vᵀ).
Why the smallest singular vector?
\(\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_i\| = \sigma_i\) for each right singular vector \(\mathbf{v}_i\) (unit length). Any unit x is a mix of the \(\mathbf{v}_i\), and \(\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\|^2 = \sum \sigma_i^2 c_i^2\) with \(\sum c_i^2 = 1\). This is smallest when all the weight goes on the smallest \(\sigma\). If \(\sigma_{\min} = 0\), it's an exact solution (no noise).

This one tool solves all three problems in this lecture: triangulation (AX = 0), F estimation (Af = 0), and finding the epipole (Fe = 0).

23The full Eight-Point Algorithm

  1. (Normalize points): translate each image's points so their centroid is at the origin, and scale so the average distance is √2 (similarity transforms T, T′).
  2. Construct the M × 9 matrix A (one row per correspondence).
  3. Find the SVD of A.
  4. Entries of F are the elements of the column of V corresponding to the least singular value. Reshape the 9-vector into 3×3.
  5. (Enforce rank 2 constraint on F)
  6. (Un-normalize F): \(\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{T}'^\top\hat{\mathbf{F}}\mathbf{T}\).

The slides mark steps 0, 4 and 5 in green as optional refinements ("see Hartley–Zisserman for why we do this"). In practice they matter a lot. Normalization keeps the matrix well-conditioned, because raw pixel products like x′x ≈ 10⁵ dwarf the 1s in the last column.

Why rank 2?

A true F has rank 2 (det F = 0), since \([\mathbf{t}]_\times\) has rank 2. With noisy data, the SVD answer is almost always rank 3. Then the epipolar lines don't all meet at a single epipole (Fe = 0 has no solution).

Enforcing rank constraints (how do we do this? SVD!)

Problem → Solution
Problem: given a matrix F, find the matrix F′ of rank k that is closest to F:
\[ \min_{\mathbf{F}',\ \text{rank}(\mathbf{F}') = k}\ \|\mathbf{F} - \mathbf{F}'\|^2 \]
Solution: compute the SVD \(\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{U}\Sigma\mathbf{V}^\top\). Form \(\Sigma'\) by replacing all but the k largest singular values with 0. Then
\[ \mathbf{F}' = \mathbf{U}\Sigma'\mathbf{V}^\top \]
For F: k = 2, so set σ₃ = 0: \(\ \text{diag}(\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3) \to \text{diag}(\sigma_1, \sigma_2, 0)\).
Mini example
Suppose SVD of a noisy F gives Σ = diag(12.0, 7.5, 0.03). The third value is tiny but not zero (that's the noise). Set it to 0: Σ′ = diag(12.0, 7.5, 0). Then F′ = UΣ′Vᵀ has rank 2, and all its epipolar lines now pass exactly through one epipole.

25Where is the epipole?

In the Arc de Triomphe image, the coloured epipolar lines converge toward a point outside the image (off to the side). Slide question: how would you compute it?

\[ \mathbf{F}\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0} \qquad\text{the epipole is in the right null space of } \mathbf{F} \]

Slide question: how would you solve for the epipole? SVD! Take \(\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{U}\Sigma\mathbf{V}^\top\). e is the last column of V (singular value 0 after the rank-2 fix). Divide by its third entry to get pixel coordinates. (For e′: the last column of U, since \(\mathbf{e}'^\top\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}\).)

"SVDs are pretty useful, huh?"
Triangulation, F estimation, rank enforcement and epipole finding: all four steps in this lecture are one SVD call.

26Python: triangulation, 8-point, epipole

import numpy as np

# ---------- Triangulation (Sections 7–8) ----------
def triangulate(P1, P2, x1, x2):
    """P1,P2: 3x4 cameras; x1,x2: (x,y) pixel matches. Returns 3D point."""
    A = np.vstack([
        x1[1]*P1[2] - P1[1],     # y p3ᵀ - p2ᵀ
        P1[0] - x1[0]*P1[2],     # p1ᵀ - x p3ᵀ
        x2[1]*P2[2] - P2[1],
        P2[0] - x2[0]*P2[2],
    ])                            # 4x4
    _, _, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A)
    X = Vt[-1]                    # smallest singular vector
    return X[:3] / X[3]

# ---------- Normalized 8-point algorithm (Sections 21–23) ----------
def normalize(pts):
    c = pts.mean(0)
    s = np.sqrt(2) / np.mean(np.linalg.norm(pts - c, axis=1))
    T = np.array([[s, 0, -s*c[0]], [0, s, -s*c[1]], [0, 0, 1]])
    ph = np.c_[pts, np.ones(len(pts))] @ T.T
    return ph, T

def eight_point(x1, x2):
    """x1, x2: (M,2) matched pixels, M >= 8. Returns F with x2ᵀ F x1 = 0."""
    p1, T1 = normalize(x1)                         # step 0
    p2, T2 = normalize(x2)
    A = np.array([[u2*u1, u2*v1, u2, v2*u1, v2*v1, v2, u1, v1, 1]
                  for (u1, v1, _), (u2, v2, _) in zip(p1, p2)])  # step 1
    _, _, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A)                    # step 2
    F = Vt[-1].reshape(3, 3)                       # step 3
    U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(F)                    # step 4: rank 2
    F = U @ np.diag([S[0], S[1], 0]) @ Vt
    F = T2.T @ F @ T1                              # step 5: un-normalize
    return F / F[2, 2]

# ---------- Epipole (Section 25) ----------
def epipoles(F):
    U, _, Vt = np.linalg.svd(F)
    e  = Vt[-1];  e  = e / e[2]     # F e = 0   (image 1)
    ep = U[:, -1]; ep = ep / ep[2]  # e'ᵀ F = 0 (image 2)
    return e, ep

# Epipolar line in image 2 for a pixel x in image 1:
# l2 = F @ np.array([x, y, 1.0])   -> a*x' + b*y' + c = 0

27Cheat sheet & self-test

  • Triangulation: known P, P′, x, x′ → X. Backproject (C and P⁺x) to get rays, but noisy rays don't meet, so solve least squares.
  • x = αPX (up to scale) → x × PX = 0 → 3 rows, only 2 independent per view → stack 2 views → 4×4 A, AX = 0 → SVD.
  • a × a = 0; a × b ⟂ a, b; \([\mathbf{a}]_\times\) is skew-symmetric; the dot product of orthogonal vectors = 0.
  • Baseline joins the camera centers · epipole = image of the other camera's center = where the baseline hits the image · epipolar plane = baseline + X · epipolar line = epipolar plane ∩ image.
  • All epipolar lines pass through the epipole. Converging cameras: epipole may lie outside the image. Parallel cameras: epipole at ∞, lines horizontal.
  • Epipolar constraint: the match of x lies on l′, so the search goes from 2D to 1D (stereo).
  • Line l = [a, b, c]; x on l ⇔ xᵀl = 0.
  • E: l′ = Ex, x′ᵀEx = 0, E = R[t]ₓ (rigid motion + coplanarity), camera coords (K = I), Longuet-Higgins 1981.
  • E: point → line (1 eq/match). H: point → point (2 eq/match).
  • E props: l′ = Ex, l = Eᵀx′, Ee = 0, e′ᵀE = 0.
  • F = K′⁻ᵀ E K⁻¹, pixel coords, x′ᵀFx = 0, depends on intrinsics + extrinsics, rank 2.
  • 8-point: 9 unknowns − scale = 8, 1 equation per match → ≥ 8 matches. Steps: normalize → A (M×9) → SVD → last col of V → rank-2 (zero σ₃) → un-normalize.
  • TLS: min ‖Ax‖² s.t. ‖x‖ = 1 → smallest right singular vector.
  • Epipole: Fe = 0 → last column of V of F.
Q1. Why can't you triangulate from a single image?A single pixel backprojects to a whole ray; every point on it projects to the same x. Depth is unknown, so you need a second view's ray.
Q2. Why don't the two backprojected rays intersect in practice?Noise in pixel positions and calibration makes them skew lines. So we find the best-fit X with least squares (SVD on AX = 0).
Q3. Why does x × PX = 0 give only 2 independent equations?The third row is a linear combination of the first two (−(x·row1 + y·row2)), so it adds nothing new.
Q4. Size of A in two-view triangulation, and how do you solve it?4×4 (2 rows per view, each row 1×4). Solve AX = 0 with SVD: X = last column of V, then divide by its 4th entry.
Q5. Define epipole. Where is it for parallel cameras?The projection of the other camera's center onto the image plane (where the baseline intersects the image). For parallel cameras it is at infinity, so the epipolar lines are parallel.
Q6. How does the epipolar constraint help stereo matching?The match for x must lie on the epipolar line l′ = Fx, so the search is 1D along a line instead of over the entire image.
Q7. Derive x′ᵀEx = 0 in three lines.Rigid motion x′ = R(x − t) ⇒ x − t = Rᵀx′. Coplanarity: (x − t)ᵀ(t × x) = 0 ⇒ x′ᵀR[t]ₓx = 0 ⇒ x′ᵀEx = 0 with E = R[t]ₓ.
Q8. Essential vs Fundamental matrix?E works on camera (normalized) coordinates, depends only on R, t, and needs known K. F works on pixel coordinates, includes K and K′: F = K′⁻ᵀEK⁻¹. F can be estimated from matches alone.
Q9. Essential matrix vs homography?Both 3×3. E maps a point to a line (l′ = Ex, 1 equation per match). H maps a point to a point (x′ = Hx, 2 equations per match).
Q10. Why 8 points, when homography needs only 4?F has 9 entries but is up to scale, so 8 unknowns. Each match gives only 1 scalar equation (x′ᵀFx = 0), so 8 matches. H gets 2 equations per match, so 8/2 = 4.
Q11. How do you enforce rank 2 on F, and why?SVD F = UΣVᵀ, set the smallest singular value to 0, recompute UΣ′Vᵀ. It's needed because a true F is singular (all epipolar lines meet at one epipole); noise makes the estimate rank 3.
Q12. Given F, how do you find the epipole e?Solve Fe = 0: e is the right null space of F, which is the last column of V from the SVD of F. Divide by e₃.
