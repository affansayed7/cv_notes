Topic 01

Spatial Frequencies

Definition — memorise this

Spatial frequency is the rate of change of intensity per unit distance in an image. Regions where intensity changes rapidly over a small area contain high spatial frequencies; regions where intensity varies slowly over many pixels contain low spatial frequencies.

Image contentFrequencyDescription
Radical change in intensity (abrupt over small areas)High frequencyfine detail, edges, texture
Slowly varying change in intensityLow frequencysmooth regions, general gray-level appearance
Topic 02

Complexity of Convolution

Definition — memorise this

The computational cost of spatial convolution is the number of multiply-add operations required to slide a kernel across an image. Filtering an M×N image with an m×n kernel costs on the order of M·N·m·n operations, because each of the M·N output pixels requires m·n multiplications.

Spatial filtering (Module 03) is a convolution operation — average filters, Gaussian filters, median filters, gradient masks, Laplacian masks are all applied by sliding a kernel. The cost:

Cost of spatial convolution $$ \text{operations} \;\propto\; M\cdot N\cdot m\cdot n $$

Each output pixel (there are M·N of them) requires one sum of m·n products. For example, a 3×3 kernel over the image below computes each output pixel as a sum of 9 products:

One output pixel, 3×3 kernel [1 0 −1; 1 0 −1; 1 0 −1] $$ (1{\times}1)+(8{\times}1)+(2{\times}1)+(2{\times}0)+(9{\times}0)+(5{\times}0)+(7{\times}{-}1)+(3{\times}{-}1)+(1{\times}{-}1)=0 $$
The problem

When the kernel is large (e.g. 201×201), m·n becomes huge, and spatial convolution becomes extremely slow. This is the motivation for the frequency domain.

Topic 03

Why Go to the Frequency Domain?

Definition — memorise this

Filtering in the frequency domain replaces the expensive spatial convolution with a simple point-wise multiplication of Fourier transforms. It takes on the order of 2MN·log₂(MN) operations using the FFT, which is dramatically faster than direct convolution for large kernels.

Frequency-domain filtering cost (via FFT) $$ \text{operations} \;\propto\; 2MN\log_2(MN) $$

The headline example

Filtering a bank of images of size 2048 × 2048 with a kernel of 201 × 201 elements:

MethodTime
Frequency domain (FFT)≈ 1 minute
Spatial convolution≈ 17 hours
The whole reason this chapter exists

For small kernels, spatial convolution is fine. For large kernels, transforming to the frequency domain, multiplying, and transforming back is enormously faster — because convolution in space becomes multiplication in frequency (the Convolution Theorem, Topic 15).

Topic 04 · Fourier Foundations

The Core Idea & Complex Exponentials

Definition — memorise this

The Fourier transform lets us describe a signal (or image) as a sum of complex exponentials, each of a different spatial frequency. Each complex exponential is a basis function of the form e^(jθ) = cos θ + j·sin θ (Euler's formula), representing a single pure frequency component.

The single most important building block is Euler's formula, which links the complex exponential to sines and cosines:

Euler's formula — the bridge $$ e^{j\theta} = \cos\theta + j\sin\theta \qquad\text{where } j=\sqrt{-1} $$

A 2D complex exponential signal of frequencies (ω₁, ω₂) is the 2D basis function of the Fourier transform:

2D complex exponential (Fourier basis function) $$ e^{\,j(\omega_1 n_1 + \omega_2 n_2)} = \cos(\omega_1 n_1 + \omega_2 n_2) + j\sin(\omega_1 n_1 + \omega_2 n_2) $$
  • These signals are periodic — they repeat, which is why they can represent the repeating patterns (frequencies) in an image.
  • A low ω = a slowly varying wave (low frequency); a high ω = a rapidly oscillating wave (high frequency).

2D complex exponentials are periodic

A crucial property: the 2D complex exponential is periodic in both directions. e^{j(ω₁(n₁+N₁)+ω₂n₂)} = e^{j(ω₁n₁+ω₂n₂)} whenever ω₁N₁ is a multiple of 2π. This periodicity is what lets a finite set of these basis functions represent any repeating pattern in an image — and it is why the discrete transform (Topic 08) only needs a finite number of frequencies.

Topic 04b

The Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT)

Definition — memorise this

The Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) expresses a signal as a sum of cosine functions of different frequencies. Unlike the Fourier transform it uses only real-valued cosines (no imaginary part), which makes it especially useful for image compression (it is the core of JPEG).

1D discrete cosine

Start from a simple 1D cosine cos(ωt). As the frequency ω increases from 0 → π/8 → π/4 → … the cosine oscillates faster. A signal is built as a weighted sum of these cosines at different frequencies:

1D DCT (analysis) $$ X(k)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x(n)\cos\!\left[\frac{\pi}{N}\left(n+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)k\right], \qquad k=0,1,\dots,N-1 $$
Period–frequency relationship (memorise this)

The period N (number of samples per full cycle) of a discrete cosine cos(ωn) is:

Period from frequency$$ N=\frac{2\pi}{\omega} $$
ωπ/8π/4π/2π
N = 2π/ω16842

Higher ω → smaller period N → the cosine oscillates faster (fewer samples per cycle). At ω=π, N=2 — the fastest a discrete signal can alternate (+,−,+,−…).

2D discrete cosine

Extending to images, the 2D DCT uses 2D cosine basis patterns (a grid of increasingly fine checkerboard-like cosine patterns). Each image block is written as a weighted sum of these patterns:

2D DCT $$ X(k_1,k_2)=\sum_{n_1=0}^{N_1-1}\sum_{n_2=0}^{N_2-1} x(n_1,n_2)\cos\!\left[\frac{\pi}{N_1}\!\left(n_1+\tfrac12\right)k_1\right]\cos\!\left[\frac{\pi}{N_2}\!\left(n_2+\tfrac12\right)k_2\right] $$
Why the DCT matters — JPEG

Natural images have most of their energy in the low-frequency cosine patterns. The DCT concentrates that energy into a few coefficients (the top-left of the block), so the high-frequency coefficients can be quantized away with little visible loss. This "energy compaction" is exactly why JPEG uses the 8×8 DCT. The DCT uses only real cosines (no j), which is cheaper and avoids complex arithmetic.

Topic 05

Fourier Series & Fourier Transform

Definition — memorise this

The Fourier series represents a periodic function as a sum of sines and cosines of different frequencies, each multiplied by a coefficient. The Fourier transform generalizes this to non-periodic functions (of finite area), representing them as an integral of sines and cosines weighted by a weighing function.

Applies toRepresentation
Fourier Seriesperiodic functionssum of sines/cosines of different frequencies × coefficients
Fourier Transformnon-periodic functions (finite area under the curve)integral of sines/cosines × a weighing function
Historical note (often a 1-mark question)

Joseph Fourier (1768–1830) developed the theory to model heat-flow problems. Both the series and the transform share the key property that a function can be reconstructed completely via an inverse process, with no loss of information — this is why we can work in the Fourier domain and return to the original domain without losing anything.

Topic 06

The Continuous Fourier Transform

Definition — memorise this

The continuous Fourier transform converts a function from the spatial (or time) domain into the frequency domain, expressing it as a continuous spectrum of frequency components. Its inverse reconstructs the original function exactly from that spectrum.

1D continuous Fourier transform pair

Forward (analysis) and inverse (synthesis) $$ F(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t)\,e^{-j\omega t}\,dt \qquad f(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} F(\omega)\,e^{\,j\omega t}\,d\omega $$

The forward transform "measures how much of frequency ω is present" by correlating f(t) with the complex exponential e^(−jωt). The inverse rebuilds f(t) by summing (integrating) all those frequency components back together.

2D continuous Fourier transform (for images)

2D forward transform $$ F(u,v)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x,y)\,e^{-j2\pi(ux+vy)}\,dx\,dy $$

where u, v are the frequency variables corresponding to the spatial coordinates x, y.

The problem with the continuous FT

The main disadvantage of the continuous FT is that it is not computable on a computer — it requires values to be computed at all possible frequencies (a continuum). This is exactly why we need the discrete version (the DFT, Topic 08).

Topic 07

Worked Example: Fourier Transform of a Kernel

Definition — memorise this

The frequency response of a spatial filter is the Fourier transform of its kernel. For a symmetric kernel, the transform reduces to a real-valued sum of cosine terms, one per distinct coefficient offset, which describes how the filter attenuates or passes each frequency.

This is the type of numerical you can be asked: "find the frequency response H(ω₁, ω₂) of the given kernel."

Example A — the plus-shaped (5-point) smoothing kernel

A simple separable-looking blur with a heavy centre and its 4 edge-neighbours:

Given kernel $$ h(n_1,n_2)=\begin{bmatrix} 0 & \tfrac{1}{6} & 0\\[2pt] \tfrac{1}{6} & \tfrac{1}{3} & \tfrac{1}{6}\\[2pt] 0 & \tfrac{1}{6} & 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

Take the DTFT — only 5 non-zero terms. The centre 1/3 contributes a constant; each opposite pair of 1/6 neighbours combines via e^{jθ}+e^{-jθ}=2\cos θ:

Frequency response $$ H(\omega_1,\omega_2)=\tfrac{1}{3} + \tfrac{1}{6}\cdot 2\cos\omega_1 + \tfrac{1}{6}\cdot 2\cos\omega_2 = \tfrac{1}{3} + \tfrac{1}{3}\cos\omega_1 + \tfrac{1}{3}\cos\omega_2 $$
Is it a smoothing or a sharpening filter?

Check the coefficient sum: 1/3 + 4×(1/6) = 1/3 + 2/3 = 1 → it passes the average (DC) unchanged. At DC (ω₁=ω₂=0): H = 1/3 + 1/3 + 1/3 = 1 (maximum). At the highest frequency (ω₁=ω₂=π): H = 1/3 − 1/3 − 1/3 = −1/3 (small/attenuated). So H is high at low frequencies and low at high frequencies → this is a blur (lowpass/smoothing) filter. Its |H| plot is a dome peaked at the centre.

Example B — the 3×3 Gaussian kernel

Same method, a fuller symmetric smoothing kernel h(n₁, n₂) (a discrete Gaussian):

Given kernel $$ h(n_1,n_2)=\begin{bmatrix} 0.075 & 0.124 & 0.075\\ 0.124 & 0.204 & 0.124\\ 0.075 & 0.124 & 0.075 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step-by-step: take the DTFT of the kernel

The frequency response is H(ω₁,ω₂) = Σ Σ h(n₁,n₂)·e^(−j(ω₁n₁+ω₂n₂)). Group the 9 terms by their position offsets and use e^(jθ)+e^(−jθ) = 2cos θ:

  • Centre (offset 0,0): contributes 0.204
  • Left & right neighbours (offsets ±1 in n₁): 0.124(e^(−jω₁)+e^(jω₁)) = 0.124·2cos ω₁
  • Top & bottom neighbours (offsets ±1 in n₂): 0.124·2cos ω₂
  • Diagonal corners (offsets ±1,±1 same sign): 0.075·2cos(ω₁+ω₂)
  • Anti-diagonal corners (offsets ±1,∓1): 0.075·2cos(ω₁−ω₂)
Result — the frequency response $$ H(\omega_1,\omega_2)=0.204 + 0.124\cdot2\cos\omega_1 + 0.124\cdot2\cos\omega_2 + 0.075\cdot2\cos(\omega_1+\omega_2) + 0.075\cdot2\cos(\omega_1-\omega_2) $$
What the answer tells you

At ω₁=ω₂=0 (zero frequency / DC), all cosines = 1, so H = 0.204 + 0.248 + 0.248 + 0.15 + 0.15 = 1 — the filter passes the average brightness unchanged (coefficients sum to 1). At high frequencies the cosines go negative, so H drops toward 0 — confirming it is a lowpass (smoothing) filter. The plot of |H| is a dome, high at the centre (low freq) and falling toward the edges (high freq).

Topic 08 · Discrete & Fast Transforms

The 2D Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)

Definition — memorise this

The Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) is the computable, sampled version of the Fourier transform: it evaluates the transform only at a finite set of equally spaced frequencies. For an image of size N₁×N₂ it produces N₁×N₂ frequency coefficients, and the inverse DFT reconstructs the original image exactly with no loss of information.

Why the DFT is needed: the continuous FT requires values at all possible frequencies — impossible to compute. So we sample at equally spaced frequencies. For an image of size N₁ × N₂, we set:

Sampling the continuous transform $$ X(k_1,k_2) = X(\omega_1,\omega_2)\Big|_{\omega_1=\frac{2\pi}{N_1}k_1,\ \omega_2=\frac{2\pi}{N_2}k_2}, \qquad k_1=0,\dots,N_1-1,\ \ k_2=0,\dots,N_2-1 $$

The 2D DFT (forward)

2D DFT $$ X(k_1,k_2)=\sum_{n_1=0}^{N_1-1}\sum_{n_2=0}^{N_2-1} x(n_1,n_2)\,e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N_1}k_1 n_1}\,e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N_2}k_2 n_2} $$

The 2D Inverse DFT

2D IDFT — reconstructs the image exactly $$ x(n_1,n_2)=\frac{1}{N_1 N_2}\sum_{k_1=0}^{N_1-1}\sum_{k_2=0}^{N_2-1} X(k_1,k_2)\,e^{+j\frac{2\pi}{N_1}k_1 n_1}\,e^{+j\frac{2\pi}{N_2}k_2 n_2} $$
Forward vs Inverse — spot the three differences

(1) The sign of the exponent flips (−j → +j). (2) The inverse has a 1/(N₁N₂) normalization factor. (3) The forward sums over n (space) to give X(k); the inverse sums over k (frequency) to give back x(n). Everything else is identical.

Topic 09

The Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)

Definition — memorise this

The Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is an efficient algorithm for computing the DFT. It avoids redundant computation by decomposing the 2D DFT into a sequence of 1D DFTs (row-wise then column-wise), reducing the cost from the direct-computation order of N⁴ down to a far smaller order — the basis of all practical frequency-domain image processing.

A — Direct computation (the slow way)

Computing the 2D DFT directly from its definition: for each output frequency (k₁,k₂), the double sum needs N₁·N₂ multiplications. There are N₁·N₂ output frequencies. So:

Direct-computation complexity $$ \text{Total} = (N_1 N_2)\cdot(N_1 N_2) = N_1^2 N_2^2 = O(N^4) \quad\text{if } N_1=N_2=N $$

B — Row-wise (row–column) decomposition = the FFT idea

The key trick: the 2D DFT separates. Split the double exponential and do the inner sum over n₂ first (a 1D DFT along each row), giving an intermediate array G(n₁,k₂), then do the outer sum over n₁ (a 1D DFT down each column):

Step 1 — 1D DFT along each row → G(n₁,k₂) $$ X(k_1,k_2)=\sum_{n_1=0}^{N_1-1}\underbrace{\left[\sum_{n_2=0}^{N_2-1} x(n_1,n_2)\,e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N_2}k_2 n_2}\right]}_{G(n_1,k_2)}\,e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N_1}k_1 n_1} $$
Step 2 — 1D DFT down each column of G $$ X(k_1,k_2)=\sum_{n_1=0}^{N_1-1} G(n_1,k_2)\,e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N_1}k_1 n_1} $$
Row-column complexity $$ \text{RC cost}=N_1\cdot(N_2)^2 + N_2\cdot(N_1)^2 = O(N^3)\quad\text{if } N_1=N_2=N $$

Going from O(N⁴) (direct) to O(N³) (row–column separation) is already a huge saving. But the row–column trick only reduces a 2D DFT to many 1D DFTs — each 1D DFT is still O(N²). The true FFT (the Cooley–Tukey algorithm) speeds up each 1D DFT from O(N²) to O(N·log₂N). Here is exactly how.

C — How the 1D FFT actually works (Cooley–Tukey)

Start from the 1D DFT and introduce the standard shorthand called the twiddle factor W_N:

1D DFT with the twiddle factor $$ X(k)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x(n)\,e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N}kn}=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x(n)\,W_N^{\,kn}, \qquad W_N = e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N}} $$

Step 1 — Split the sum into even and odd samples. Assume N is a power of 2. Separate the input index n into even indices n=2m and odd indices n=2m+1:

Split into even + odd $$ X(k)=\sum_{m=0}^{N/2-1} x(2m)\,W_N^{\,k(2m)} \;+\; \sum_{m=0}^{N/2-1} x(2m+1)\,W_N^{\,k(2m+1)} $$

Step 2 — Use the key twiddle-factor identity W_N^{2} = W_{N/2}. Since W_N^{2km} = e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N}2km} = e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N/2}km} = W_{N/2}^{\,km}, and factoring W_N^{k} out of the odd sum:

Two half-size DFTs appear $$ X(k)=\underbrace{\sum_{m=0}^{N/2-1} x(2m)\,W_{N/2}^{\,km}}_{E(k)\ =\ \text{DFT of even samples}} \;+\; W_N^{\,k}\underbrace{\sum_{m=0}^{N/2-1} x(2m+1)\,W_{N/2}^{\,km}}_{O(k)\ =\ \text{DFT of odd samples}} $$

So an N-point DFT is built from two N/2-point DFTs — one over the even-indexed samples (E(k)) and one over the odd-indexed samples (O(k)). This is the divide step.

Step 3 — The butterfly (combine step). We only computed E(k) and O(k) for k = 0 … N/2−1. To get the upper half of the spectrum, use the periodicity of the half-size DFTs (E(k+N/2)=E(k), O(k+N/2)=O(k)) and the identity W_N^{\,k+N/2} = -W_N^{\,k}:

The butterfly equations $$ X(k) = E(k) + W_N^{\,k}\,O(k) $$ $$ X\!\left(k+\tfrac{N}{2}\right) = E(k) - W_N^{\,k}\,O(k) \qquad k=0,1,\dots,\tfrac{N}{2}-1 $$

Each pair of outputs is computed from the same E(k), O(k), and one twiddle multiply — a "+" and a "−". Drawn as a signal-flow graph, this crossing pattern looks like a butterfly:

Step 4 — Recurse. Each N/2-point DFT splits into two N/4-point DFTs, and so on, until we reach trivial 1-point DFTs. There are log₂N stages, and each stage does N/2 butterflies (≈ N operations). This is the whole speed-up:

1D FFT complexity $$ \underbrace{\log_2 N}_{\text{stages}} \times \underbrace{\tfrac{N}{2}\ \text{butterflies}}_{\approx\, N\ \text{ops/stage}} \;=\; O(N\log_2 N) \quad\text{vs. } O(N^2)\text{ for the direct DFT} $$

Worked complexity comparison — N = 1024

Direct DFT: N² = 1024² ≈ 1.05 × 10⁶ operations. FFT: N·log₂N = 1024 × 10 ≈ 1.0 × 10⁴ operations. That's roughly a 100× speed-up for a 1024-point transform — and the gap grows as N increases (for N=10⁶, it's a ~50,000× speed-up).

The FFT in one breath (for the exam)

Split the DFT sum into even and odd indexed samples → each becomes a half-size DFT (E, O) using W_N² = W_{N/2} → recombine with the butterfly X(k)=E(k)+W_N^k O(k), X(k+N/2)=E(k)−W_N^k O(k) using W_N^{k+N/2}=−W_N^k → recurse for log₂N stages → total O(N log₂N). For 2D images, apply this 1D FFT to every row, then every column.

Topic 10

Algorithm Complexity

Definition — memorise this

The FFT reduces the complexity of computing an N-point (or N×N) DFT from the order of N² (or N⁴ in 2D, direct) down to the order of N·log₂N (or 2N²·log₂N in 2D), which is the reason large-scale frequency-domain processing is practical.

Method2D complexity (N×N image)
Direct DFT computationO(N⁴)
Row–column decompositionO(N³)
Full FFT≈ 2N²·log₂N

The FFT algorithm was developed in the 1960s for seismic exploration. Improvements reduced the DFT complexity to 2N²·log₂N. For N=256: 2N²·log₂N ≈ 10⁶ operations — a few seconds on a workstation, versus the vastly larger direct-computation cost.

Topic 11 · Properties of the DFT

Shifting & Centering the DFT

Definition — memorise this

Multiplying an image by (−1)^(x+y) before computing its DFT shifts the origin (the zero-frequency / DC component) of the transform to the centre of the frequency rectangle, at (M/2, N/2). This "centering" makes the spectrum easier to view and to filter, since low frequencies gather in the middle.

The centering (shift) property $$ f(x,y)\;\longleftrightarrow\;F(u,v) $$ $$ f(x,y)\cdot(-1)^{x+y}\;\longleftrightarrow\;F\!\left(u-\tfrac{M}{2},\,v-\tfrac{N}{2}\right) $$

It is common practice to multiply the input image by (−1)^(x+y) prior to computing the FT. This shifts the centre of the transform to (M/2, N/2).

Topic 12

Symmetry & the Spectrum

Definition — memorise this

For a real-valued image, the Fourier transform is conjugate symmetric: F(u,v) = F*(−u,−v). Consequently, the Fourier spectrum (the magnitude |F(u,v)|) is symmetric about the origin: |F(u,v)| = |F(−u,−v)|.

Conjugate symmetry (real image) $$ F(u,v)=F^{*}(-u,-v) $$
Magnitude (spectrum) is symmetric $$ |F(u,v)|=|F(-u,-v)| $$

The three descriptors of the transform

Since F(u,v) is complex, we describe it with:

Spectrum, phase, and power $$ |F(u,v)|=\sqrt{R^2+I^2}\ \text{(magnitude / spectrum)} \qquad \phi(u,v)=\tan^{-1}\!\frac{I}{R}\ \text{(phase angle)} $$ $$ P(u,v)=|F(u,v)|^2=R^2+I^2 \ \text{(power spectrum)} $$

where R and I are the real and imaginary parts of F(u,v).

Why we log-transform the spectrum for display

A Fourier spectrum has an enormous dynamic range (the DC term dwarfs everything). To make it visible we apply D(u,v)=c·log(1+|F(u,v)|) — the log transform from Module 02. The "computing spectrum" figure shows: a rectangle image → its spectrum (tiny bright spots in the corners) → the centered spectrum → the log-transformed spectrum (now the detail is visible).

Topic 13

Translation & Rotation Properties

Definition — memorise this

Translating an image in space does not change the magnitude of its Fourier spectrum (only its phase); rotating an image by an angle rotates its Fourier spectrum by the same angle. These properties show that the spectrum's shape depends on the image's frequency content and orientation, not on its position.

Spatial operationEffect on the Fourier transform
Translation (shift the image by x₀, y₀)only the phase changes (multiplied by a phase term); the magnitude spectrum |F| is unchanged
Rotation (rotate the image by θ)the spectrum rotates by the same angle θ
Translation property $$ f(x-x_0,\,y-y_0)\;\longleftrightarrow\;F(u,v)\,e^{-j2\pi\left(\frac{ux_0}{M}+\frac{vy_0}{N}\right)} $$
The intuition

Moving an object around an image doesn't change which frequencies are present (edges are still edges, textures still textures) — only where they align, which lives in the phase. But rotating the object rotates its edges/textures, so the frequency content rotates with it.

Topic 14

Low vs High Frequency Content

Definition — memorise this

In the Fourier spectrum, the low-frequency components (near the centre of a centered spectrum) are responsible for the general gray-level appearance of an image, while the high-frequency components (away from the centre) are responsible for its fine detail such as edges and texture.

Spectrum regionFrequenciesResponsible for
Centre (of centered spectrum)low frequencygeneral gray-level appearance — the smooth, overall look
Away from centrehigh frequencydetail — edges, sharp transitions, fine texture
The reconstruction demo

Keeping progressively more of the spectrum (e.g. 5% → 10% → 20% → 50% of the coefficients, from the centre outward) and inverse-transforming shows the image going from very blurred to nearly perfect — visual proof that low frequencies carry the bulk of the appearance and high frequencies add the crisp detail.

Topic 15 · Filtering

The Convolution Theorem

Definition — memorise this

The Convolution Theorem states that convolution in the spatial domain is equivalent to multiplication in the frequency domain, and vice versa. That is, if g = f ∗ h (convolution), then its Fourier transform is G = F·H (point-wise product).

The convolution theorem (both directions) $$ g = f * h \;\;\longleftrightarrow\;\; G = F\cdot H $$ $$ g = f\cdot h \;\;\longleftrightarrow\;\; G = F * H $$

where F, H, G are the Fourier transforms of f, h, g respectively.

How you actually use it

To filter g = f ∗ h without doing the expensive convolution: (1) take the FT of both — F and H; (2) multiply them point-wise — G = F·H; (3) take the inverse FT of G to get g. For large kernels this three-step route is far cheaper than direct convolution (Topic 03).

Numerical intuition — noise removal example

A signal = sum of 3 sinusoids (20, 50, 80 Hz) + random noise. Filtering in the time domain with a length-5 averaging filter [1 1 1 1 1]/5 smooths it but blurs the peaks. Filtering in the frequency domain with a cut-off at 90 Hz cleanly removes the high-frequency noise while keeping the three sinusoids intact — a direct demonstration of why frequency-domain filtering is so precise for frequency-selective tasks.

Topic 16

The Frequency-Domain Filtering Pipeline

Definition — memorise this

Frequency-domain filtering enhances an image by: transforming it to the frequency domain with the FT, multiplying by a filter function H(u,v), and transforming the result back with the inverse FT. The enhanced image is g(x,y) = Real{ ℱ⁻¹[ H(u,v)·F(u,v) ] }.

The complete filtering equation $$ g(x,y)=\text{Real}\Big\{\,\mathcal{F}^{-1}\big[\,H(u,v)\,F(u,v)\,\big]\Big\} $$
Choosing H(u,v) decides the filter

The filter function H(u,v) is where you decide what to keep: a lowpass H (passes the centre, blocks the edges) smooths/blurs; a highpass H (blocks the centre, passes the edges) sharpens / extracts edges. This is the frequency-domain counterpart of the spatial smoothing vs sharpening filters from Module 03 — connected by the Convolution Theorem.

Topic 17 · Filter Design

Steps of Performing Frequency-Domain Filtering

Definition — memorise this

The standard frequency-domain filtering procedure pads the image, centers its transform, multiplies by a filter transfer function H(u,v), inverse-transforms, and crops back to the original size. Padding to P=2M, Q=2N is required to prevent wraparound (circular-convolution) errors.

The 8-step procedure (Gonzalez & Woods)

  1. Given an input image f(x,y) of size M×N, obtain padding sizes P = 2M and Q = 2N.
  2. Form a padded image f_p(x,y) of size P×Q using zero-, mirror-, or replicate-padding.
  3. Multiply f_p(x,y) by (−1)^{x+y} to center the transform on the P×Q frequency rectangle.
  4. Compute the DFT F(u,v) of the image from step 3.
  5. Construct a real, symmetric filter transfer function H(u,v) of size P×Q, centered at (P/2, Q/2).
  6. Form the product G(u,v) = H(u,v)·F(u,v) by elementwise multiplication.
  7. Obtain the filtered image (size P×Q): g_p(x,y) = Real{ℱ⁻¹[G(u,v)]}·(−1)^{x+y}.
  8. Obtain the final result g(x,y) of size M×N by extracting the top-left M×N region from g_p(x,y).
The (−1)^(x+y) appears twice

Once in step 3 (to center the spectrum before filtering) and again in step 7 (to undo the centering after the inverse transform). Both are needed for the result to come out correct and right-side-up.

Topic 18

The Need for Padding

Definition — memorise this

Multiplication in the frequency domain corresponds to circular (periodic) convolution in the spatial domain. Because both functions are treated as periodic, without padding the tail of one period wraps around and corrupts the other — so the image and filter are zero-padded to P=2M, Q=2N to give the linear-convolution result.

  • Because we are convolving two periodic functions, the convolution itself is periodic — adjacent periods can overlap and interfere. This is called wraparound error.
  • Multiplication in the frequency domain G = H·F corresponds to circular convolution in the spatial domain, not the linear convolution we actually want.
  • Padding both functions to size P = 2M, Q = 2N (or at least P ≥ 2M−1) separates the periods enough that the circular convolution equals the desired linear convolution.
Topic 19

Frequency-Based Filters — Overview

Definition — memorise this

A lowpass filter passes low-frequency components and attenuates high frequencies, which blurs (smooths) the image. A highpass filter passes high-frequency components and attenuates low frequencies, which sharpens the image and extracts edges.

FilterPassesBlocksEffect on image
Lowpass (LPF)low frequencies (centre)high frequencies (edges of spectrum)blurs / smooths — restricts data to low-freq components
Highpass (HPF)high frequencieslow frequenciessharpens / extracts edges

Each comes in three flavours — Ideal, Butterworth, and Gaussian — which differ in how sharply they cut off. The distance from the centre of the (centered) frequency rectangle is:

Distance from the frequency-domain centre $$ D(u,v)=\Big[(u-\tfrac{M}{2})^2+(v-\tfrac{N}{2})^2\Big]^{1/2} $$

D₀ (a chosen constant) is the cutoff frequency — the radius at which the filter transitions.

Topic 20

Ideal Lowpass Filter (ILPF)

Definition — memorise this

The Ideal Lowpass Filter passes, without attenuation, all frequencies inside a circle of radius D₀ about the origin and completely removes all frequencies outside it. It has a perfectly sharp cutoff, but this abrupt transition causes ringing artifacts in the filtered image.

ILPF transfer function $$ H(u,v)=\begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } D(u,v)\le D_0\\ 0 & \text{if } D(u,v) > D_0 \end{cases} $$

The filter is radially symmetric about the origin — a flat cylinder of radius D₀: everything inside passes untouched, everything outside is zeroed.

Ringing — the price of a sharp cutoff

Because the ILPF has a sharp discontinuity in frequency, its spatial-domain equivalent is a sinc-like function with oscillating side-lobes. Convolving with those side-lobes creates ringing and blurring around edges. The ringing is more severe the narrower the filter (the smaller D₀). This is why the ideal filter is rarely used in practice.

Topic 21

Gaussian Lowpass Filter (GLPF)

Definition — memorise this

The Gaussian Lowpass Filter has a transfer function shaped like a Gaussian, giving a smooth transition from pass to stop with no sharp discontinuity. Because its spatial-domain form is also Gaussian (no side-lobes), it produces absolutely no ringing artifacts.

GLPF transfer function $$ H(u,v)=e^{-\frac{D^2(u,v)}{2\sigma^2}} \qquad\text{or, with } \sigma=D_0:\qquad H(u,v)=e^{-\frac{D^2(u,v)}{2D_0^2}} $$

where σ is the measure of the spread of the Gaussian curve. Setting σ = D₀ makes D₀ the cutoff where H drops to e^{−1/2} ≈ 0.607 of its peak.

ILPF vs GLPF

ILPF: sharpest cutoff, but rings. GLPF: no ringing ever (guaranteed, because a Gaussian's transform is a Gaussian with no negative side-lobes), but a gentler cutoff so it blurs a bit more for the same D₀. Butterworth sits between them.

Topic 22

Butterworth Lowpass Filter (BLPF)

Definition — memorise this

The Butterworth Lowpass Filter has a smooth transfer function controlled by an order n. It has no sharp discontinuity and no single clear cutoff; a low order (n=1,2) behaves like a Gaussian with no ringing, while a high order approaches the ideal filter (sharper cutoff, but ringing reappears).

BLPF transfer function (order n, cutoff D₀) $$ H(u,v)=\frac{1}{1+\big[D(u,v)/D_0\big]^{2n}} $$

At D(u,v)=D₀, H = 1/(1+1) = 0.5 regardless of n — so D₀ is the half-power point. The order n controls the steepness of the transition.

The order n is the tuning knob

n=1: very smooth, Gaussian-like, no ringing. n=2: a good compromise (mild transition, negligible ringing) — the most commonly used. n large (e.g. 20): approaches the ILPF and ringing returns. Butterworth lets you dial between "no ringing" and "sharp cutoff".

Topic 23

Highpass Filters (Ideal, Butterworth, Gaussian)

Definition — memorise this

A highpass filter is obtained from the corresponding lowpass filter by H_hp(u,v) = 1 − H_lp(u,v). It passes high frequencies (fine detail and edges) and blocks low frequencies (the smooth background), producing a sharpened / edge-enhanced image.

The master relationship $$ H_{hp}(u,v)=1-H_{lp}(u,v) $$

Apply it to each of the three lowpass filters:

Ideal Highpass (IHPF) $$ H(u,v)=\begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } D(u,v) > D_0\\ 0 & \text{if } D(u,v)\le D_0 \end{cases} $$
Butterworth Highpass (BHPF) $$ H(u,v)=\frac{1}{1+\big[D_0/D(u,v)\big]^{2n}} $$
Gaussian Highpass (GHPF) $$ H(u,v)=1-e^{-\frac{D^2(u,v)}{2D_0^2}} $$
Highpass filterCutoff behaviourRinging?
Ideal (IHPF)brick-wall: blocks all D≤D₀, passes all D>D₀yes — strong ringing

Exam-favourite fact

Every highpass filter is just 1 − (its lowpass version). So if you can write the three lowpass transfer functions, you get the three highpass ones for free. Same ringing behaviour carries over: Ideal rings, Gaussian never rings, Butterworth is tunable.

Cram Sheet

All Definitions, In One Place

Every "Definition — memorise this" box from the topics above, gathered here for a final memorization pass before the exam.

1. Spatial Frequency

The rate of change of intensity per unit distance in an image. Rapid change over a small area = high frequency; slow change over many pixels = low frequency.

2. Complexity of Convolution

Filtering an M×N image with an m×n kernel costs on the order of M·N·m·n operations (each of M·N output pixels needs m·n multiplications) — very slow for large kernels.

3. Why the Frequency Domain

Frequency-domain filtering replaces spatial convolution with point-wise multiplication, taking on the order of 2MN·log₂(MN) operations via the FFT — dramatically faster for large kernels (e.g. 1 min vs 17 hours).

4. Fourier Idea & Complex Exponentials

The FT describes a signal as a sum of complex exponentials of different frequencies. Each basis function is e^(jθ)=cosθ+j·sinθ (Euler), one pure frequency.

5. Fourier Series & Transform

Series: a periodic function as a sum of sines/cosines × coefficients. Transform: a non-periodic (finite-area) function as an integral of sines/cosines × a weighing function.

6. Continuous Fourier Transform

Converts a function from the spatial/time domain to the frequency domain as a continuous spectrum; the inverse reconstructs it exactly. Not directly computable (needs all frequencies).

7. FT of a Kernel (frequency response)

The frequency response of a filter is the FT of its kernel; for a symmetric kernel it reduces to a real sum of cosine terms describing how each frequency is passed or attenuated.

8. 2D DFT & IDFT

The computable, sampled FT: evaluates the transform at a finite set of equally spaced frequencies. For an N₁×N₂ image it gives N₁×N₂ coefficients; the IDFT reconstructs the image exactly.

9. Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)

An efficient DFT algorithm that decomposes the 2D DFT into 1D row then column DFTs (and divide-and-conquer within each), cutting cost from O(N⁴) direct toward 2N²log₂N.

10. Algorithm Complexity

FFT reduces an N-point DFT from O(N²) to O(N·log₂N) (2D: O(N⁴) direct → 2N²·log₂N), making large-scale frequency processing practical.

11. Shifting / Centering the DFT

Multiplying the image by (−1)^(x+y) before the DFT shifts the zero-frequency (DC) term to the centre (M/2, N/2), making the spectrum easier to view and filter.

12. Symmetry & Spectrum

For a real image, F(u,v)=F*(−u,−v) (conjugate symmetric), so the magnitude spectrum is symmetric: |F(u,v)|=|F(−u,−v)|. Spectrum = magnitude, phase = angle, power = |F|².

13. Translation & Rotation

Translating an image changes only the FT phase, not the magnitude spectrum; rotating the image rotates its spectrum by the same angle.

14. Low vs High Frequency

Low-frequency components (centre of a centered spectrum) give the general gray-level appearance; high-frequency components (outer region) give the fine detail (edges, texture).

15. Convolution Theorem

Convolution in space = multiplication in frequency: g=f∗h ⟷ G=F·H (and g=f·h ⟷ G=F∗H). This is why frequency-domain filtering works.

16. Frequency-Domain Filtering

Transform to frequency, multiply by filter H(u,v), inverse-transform back: g(x,y)=Real{ℱ⁻¹[H(u,v)F(u,v)]}. Lowpass H smooths; highpass H sharpens.

04b. Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT)

Represents a signal as a sum of real-valued cosines of different frequencies. Compacts image energy into few low-frequency coefficients → basis of JPEG compression.

17. Steps of Filtering

Pad to P=2M, Q=2N → center with (−1)^(x+y) → DFT → multiply by H(u,v) → inverse DFT → undo centering → crop back to M×N.

18. Need for Padding

Multiplication in frequency = circular convolution in space; padding to 2M×2N prevents wraparound error so the result equals true linear convolution.

19. Lowpass vs Highpass

LPF passes low freq (blurs); HPF passes high freq (sharpens/edges). D(u,v)=√((u−M/2)²+(v−N/2)²); D₀ = cutoff frequency.

20. Ideal Lowpass (ILPF)

H=1 if D≤D₀ else 0. Sharp brick-wall cutoff → severe ringing (worse for smaller D₀).

21. Gaussian Lowpass (GLPF)

H=e^(−D²/2D₀²). Smooth transition → no ringing ever; gentler (more blurring) cutoff.

22. Butterworth Lowpass (BLPF)

H=1/(1+[D/D₀]^(2n)). Order n tunes sharpness: n=1–2 smooth like Gaussian, large n approaches ideal (ringing returns). H=0.5 at D₀.

23. Highpass Filters

H_hp = 1 − H_lp. Ideal (rings), Butterworth 1/(1+[D₀/D]^(2n)) (tunable), Gaussian 1−e^(−D²/2D₀²) (no ringing).
