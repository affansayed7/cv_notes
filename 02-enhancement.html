Topic 01

What Is Image Enhancement?

Enhancement = processing an image so that certain features can be derived more easily, or so overall quality improves. Typical goals: remove noise, improve contrast, highlight edges.

ApproachWhat it operates on
Spatial domaintechniques that operate directly on pixels of the image plane
Frequency domaintechniques based on modifying the Fourier transform of an image

Every spatial-domain technique is a case of the general transform g(x,y) = T[f(x,y)]. When the neighbourhood used by T is just 1×1 (a single pixel), this is called point processing, and the transform is written more simply as:

Point processing $$ s = T(r) $$
  • r: gray level at (x,y) in the original image f(x,y)
  • s: gray level at (x,y) in the processed image g(x,y)
  • T is called a gray-level transformation or mapping
Remember

This single S-curve family is the "master diagram" for point processing: a gentle S-curve is contrast stretching, and a steep, almost-vertical S-curve at a point k is thresholding — they're the same idea at different steepness.

Topic 02

Contrast Stretching & Thresholding

Both are point-processing intensity transformation functions — instances of the S-curve above, applied identically to every pixel via s = T(r).

RuleEffect
Contrast stretchinggentle piecewise-linear s=T(r)expands/compresses the intensity range — improves low-contrast images
Thresholdingr < T → one level,  r ≥ T → another levela limiting case of contrast stretching — produces a two-level (binary) image
Topic 03 · Basic Gray-Level Transforms

Image Negative

The simplest of all enhancement techniques. Given intensity levels in the range [0, L−1]:

Negative transformation $$ s = (L-1) - r $$

Reverses the intensity levels of an image — produces the equivalent of a photographic negative. Common use: medical imaging (e.g. digital mammograms), where reversed contrast makes certain features easier to see.

Topic 04

Piecewise-Linear Contrast Stretching & Gray-Level Slicing

A piecewise-linear transformation used to improve the range of image intensity values in a low-contrast image. Two control points, (r₁,s₁) and (r₂,s₂), determine the shape of the transform function.

Control pointsEffect
r₁=s₁ and r₂=s₂the transform equals the identity line → no change in intensity levels
intermediate values of (r₁,s₁), (r₂,s₂)produce varying degrees of intensity spread → different contrast

Gray-level slicing

A related piecewise transform, but the goal is different: instead of spreading the whole range, it highlights a specific band of gray levels [A, B] — e.g. to emphasise a particular feature such as a mass in an X-ray.

Exam distinction

(a) discards background — everything outside [A,B] is reduced to one low level. (b) preserves background — everything outside [A,B] is left unchanged. Contrast stretching reshapes the entire range; slicing isolates one band.

Topic 05

Logarithmic Transform

Log transformation $$ s = c\,\log(1+r) $$

Maps a narrow range of low gray-level input values into a wider range of output levels; the opposite is true for higher input values (they get compressed).

  • Used to expand dark pixel values while compressing higher-level values.
  • The inverse log transformation does the opposite.
  • Compresses the dynamic range of images with very large pixel-value variation.

Classic example · Fourier spectrum

A Fourier spectrum can have values from 0 to 10⁶. Applying s = c·log(1+r) with c=1 compresses the range to about 0–6.2, making the spectrum visible on an 8-bit display.

Topic 06

Power-Law (Gamma) Transform

Power-law transformation $$ s = c\,r^{\gamma} $$

When c = γ = 1 → identity function (no change).

  • Fractional γ (γ < 1): maps a narrow range of dark input values into a wider range of output values (opposite for higher values).
  • γ > 1: the reverse effect — compresses dark tones, expands bright tones.

Gamma correction

Many capture/display/print devices have an intensity-to-voltage response following a power law. CRT monitors, for example, have exponents roughly 1.8 – 2.5, making raw output look darker than the input.

Gamma correction compensates: a simple gray-scale linear wedge input to a monitor produces a darker-than-input output; applying s = r^{1/2.5} before display makes the monitor's output close in appearance to the original image. The same idea applies to scanners and printers — only the device-dependent γ value changes.

Remember

Correction exponent = 1/device γ. Device response r^{2.5} → correct with r^{1/2.5} = r^{0.4}. Similar analysis applies to other imaging devices (scanner, printer) — the only difference is the device-dependent value of gamma.

Topic 07

Bit-Plane Slicing

Instead of gray levels, imagine the image in terms of the bits used to represent each pixel. Suppose each pixel is represented by 8 bits — the image can be thought of as 8 binary bit-planes, plane 7 = most significant bit (MSB) of every pixel, plane 0 = least significant.

  • Higher-order bit planes (7, 6, 5…) contain the majority of visually significant data.
  • Useful for analysing the relative importance of each bit.
  • This decomposition is also useful for image compression.

Classic example

Slicing a currency note image into its 8 bit-planes: the top planes (7, 6, 5…) still clearly show the note's design, while the bottom planes (1, 0) look like pure random noise — visually confirming that most information lives in the high-order bits.

Topic 08 · Histogram-Based Enhancement

What Are Histograms?

Before applying it to images: a histogram, in general, is a bar chart of how often each value (or value-range/"bin") occurs in a dataset — bin/interval on the x-axis, count/frequency on the y-axis.

Histogram Processing (applied to images)

The histogram of a digital image with gray levels in the range [0, L−1] is a discrete function:

Image histogram $$ h(r_k) = n_k $$
  • rₖ: the k-th gray level
  • nₖ: the number of pixels in the image having gray level rₖ
  • h(rₖ): the histogram of a digital image with gray levels rₖ
Topic 09

Normalized Histogram

Dividing each histogram value at gray level rₖ by the total number of pixels in the image, n:

Normalized histogram $$ p(r_k) = \frac{n_k}{n}, \qquad k=0,1,\dots,L-1 $$
  • p(rₖ) gives an estimate of the probability of occurrence of gray level rₖ
  • The sum of all components of a normalized histogram is equal to 1
  • Total number of pixels n = N×M
Why normalize?

Raw counts nₖ depend on image size — a bigger image just has bigger counts. Dividing by n turns the histogram into a proper probability distribution, comparable across images of any size. This is exactly what makes the histogram equalization math (Topics 13–15) work.

Topic 11

Histogram Shapes & Image Type

The shape of a histogram directly tells you what kind of image you're looking at.

Histogram looks likeImage is…
concentrated on the low sidea dark image
concentrated on the high sidea light image
narrow, centred toward the middlelow-contrast image
broad, near-uniform, few tall spikeshigh-contrast image
The key insight — this motivates equalization

As a low-contrast image's histogram is narrow and centered toward the middle of the gray scale, if we distribute the histogram to a wider range, the quality of the image will be improved. Notice the relationship: the high-contrast image, of all of them, has the most evenly spaced histogram.

Topic 12 · Histogram Equalization

Histogram Equalization — The Goal

  • Increases the dynamic range of an image
  • Enhances contrast of the image to cover all possible gray levels
  • Ideal histogram = flat (equalized) — the same number of pixels at each gray level
Ideal (flat) pixel count per gray level $$ \text{ideal count at each gray level} = \frac{M \times N}{L} $$
Topic 13

Deriving T(r) as a CDF

To get the mapping of the form s=T(r), analyze the ideal, continuous case first, assuming 0 ≤ r ≤ L−1 and 0 ≤ s ≤ L−1.

Assume that T(r) is a gray-level transformation function satisfying two conditions:

  1. (a) T(r) is single-valued and monotonically increasing on 0 ≤ r ≤ L−1
  2. (b) 0 ≤ T(r) ≤ L−1 for 0 ≤ r ≤ L−1

Meaning of the two conditions

ConditionWhy it matters
(a) single-valued
(one-to-one relationship)		guarantees that the inverse transformation will exist
(a) monotonically increasingpreserves the increasing order from black to white in the output image, thus it won't cause a negative image. Not monotonic could invert at least a section of the intensity range, producing some inverted gray levels in the output
(b) 0≤T(r)≤L−1guarantees that the output gray levels will be in the same range as the input levels

The inverse transformation from s back to r is: r = T⁻¹(s), 0 ≤ s ≤ L−1.

Probability density function background

  • The intensity levels in an image may be viewed as random variables in the interval [0, L−1].
  • PDF (probability density function) is one of the fundamental descriptors of a random variable.

Random variables — transforming one PDF into another

If a random variable x is transformed by a monotonic transformation function T(x) to produce a new random variable y, the PDF of y can be obtained from knowledge of T(x) and the PDF of x:

Transformation of a random variable's PDF $$ p_y(y) = p_x(x)\left|\frac{dx}{dy}\right| $$
  • T(x) is monotonically increasing if T(x₁) < T(x₂) for x₁ < x₂
  • T(x) is monotonically decreasing if T(x₁) > T(x₂) for x₁ < x₂
  • The preceding equation is valid if T(x) is an increasing or decreasing function

Applied to the image

If pᵣ(r) and T(r) are known, and T⁻¹(s) satisfies condition (a), then pₛ(s) can be obtained using:

PDF of the output image $$ p_s(s) = p_r(r)\left|\frac{dr}{ds}\right| $$

where pᵣ(r) denotes the PDF of random variable r (input), and pₛ(s) denotes the PDF of random variable s (output). The PDF of the transformed variable s is determined by the PDF of the input image and by the chosen transformation function.

The transformation function IS a CDF

A transformation function is a cumulative distribution function (CDF) of random variable r:

T(r) defined as a CDF $$ s = T(r) = (L-1)\int_0^r p_r(w)\,dw $$

where w is a dummy variable of integration. The right side is recognised as the cumulative distribution function of random variable r. Note: T(r) depends on pᵣ(r).

Why a CDF satisfies both conditions

A CDF is an integral of a probability function (always positive) — it's the area under the curve. Thus, a CDF is always single-valued and monotonically increasing → satisfies condition (a). Similarly, the integral of a PDF for variables in the range [0, L−1] is also in the range [0, L−1] → satisfies condition (b) too.

Topic 14

Proving pₛ(s) Is Uniform

Starting from s = T(r) = (L-1)∫₀ʳ pᵣ(w)dw, differentiate with respect to r:

Step 1 — derivative of T(r) $$ \frac{ds}{dr}=\frac{dT(r)}{dr}=(L-1)\frac{d}{dr}\left[\int_0^r p_r(w)\,dw\right]=(L-1)\,p_r(r) $$

Now substitute this into pₛ(s) = pᵣ(r)|dr/ds|:

Step 2 — substitute and simplify $$ p_s(s)=p_r(r)\cdot\frac{1}{(L-1)\,p_r(r)}=\frac{1}{L-1}, \qquad 0\le s \le L-1 $$
The whole point of the derivation

Since pₛ(s) is a probability function, it must be zero outside [0, L−1] because its integral over all values of s must equal 1 (scaled: equal L−1 here since we didn't divide by it). This is called a uniform probability density function — and pₛ(s) is always uniform, independent of the form of pᵣ(r). This is exactly why histogram equalization works on any input histogram shape.

Topic 15

Discrete Histogram Equalization

Images are discrete, so we approximate the probability of occurrence of gray level rₖ:

Discrete probability $$ p_r(r_k) = \frac{n_k}{n}, \qquad k=0,1,\dots,L-1 $$

The discrete version of the transformation:

Discrete histogram equalization $$ s_k = T(r_k) = (L-1)\sum_{j=0}^{k} p_r(r_j) = (L-1)\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{n_j}{n}, \qquad k=0,1,\dots,L-1 $$

An output image is obtained by mapping each pixel with level rₖ in the input image into a corresponding pixel with level sₖ in the output image. This mapping is called Histogram Equalization or Histogram Linearization.

Important caveat

In discrete space, it cannot be proved in general that this discrete transformation will produce the discrete equivalent of a uniform probability density function (i.e. a perfectly uniform histogram) — unlike the continuous case in Topic 14. In practice the output histogram is only approximately flat, but is reliably more spread out than the input.

Recipe to memorise

1) Build histogram nₖ. 2) Normalize: nₖ/n. 3) Cumulative sum. 4) Multiply by (L−1) and round → new gray level for each old level.

Topic 18

Histogram Matching & Global vs Local Enhancement

Histogram Matching (Specification)

Histogram equalization has a disadvantage: it can generate only one type of output image. With Histogram Specification, we can specify the shape of the histogram that we wish the output image to have — it doesn't have to be a uniform histogram.

Global vs Local Enhancement

ApproachHow it works
Global (histogram processing)suitable for overall enhancement — pixels are modified by a transformation function based on the gray-level content of an entire image
Localneeded when we must enhance small areas in an image whose pixels have negligible influence on the global transformation's computation; transformation is based on gray-level distribution in the neighbourhood of every pixel

Worked example · global vs local HE

An 8-bit, 512×512 image has five black squares on a light gray background — but each square secretly hides a faint shape (a letter, a circle, some text) at very low contrast against the black.

(a) original

(b) global HE

(c) local HE (3×3)

Global HE stretches contrast for the whole image at once — the squares turn uniformly gray, but the hidden shapes inside them stay invisible. Local HE (3×3 neighbourhood) adapts to each small region separately, so the faint shapes inside each square finally become visible — at the cost of amplifying background noise/grain.

Exam one-liner

For local enhancement, the transformation should be based on the gray-level distribution in the neighbourhood of every pixel — not the whole image.

Topic 19

Histogram Statistics for Enhancement

  • Mean of gray levels in an image: a measure of darkness/brightness of the image
  • Variance of gray levels in an image: a measure of average contrast
Global mean and variance $$ m=\sum_{i=0}^{L-1} r_i\,p(r_i), \qquad \sigma^2=\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}(r_i-m)^2\,p(r_i) $$

Two uses of mean & variance

TypeUsed for
Global mean & variancemeasured over an entire image; used primarily for gross adjustments of overall intensity and contrast
Local mean & varianceused as the basis for changes that depend on image characteristics in a predefined region about each pixel

Local mean and variance — formulas

Computed over neighbourhood Sxy $$ m_{Sxy}=\sum_{(r,s)\in S_{xy}} r_{i}\,p_{Sxy}(r_i), \qquad \sigma^2_{Sxy}=\sum_{(r,s)\in S_{xy}}(r_i-m_{Sxy})^2\,p_{Sxy}(r_i) $$
  • (x,y): coordinates of a pixel in an image
  • Sxy: neighbourhood (sub-image of specified size), centered at (x,y)
  • p_Sxy(rᵢ): the histogram of pixels in region Sxy

Local enhancement method

Local enhancement rule $$ g(x,y)=\begin{cases}E\cdot f(x,y) & \text{if } m_{Sxy}\le k_0 m_G \ \text{AND}\ k_1\sigma_G \le \sigma_{Sxy} \le k_2\sigma_G\\[4pt] f(x,y) & \text{otherwise}\end{cases} $$

where m_G = global mean, σ_G = global variance, and E is an amplification factor applied only when a pixel's local neighbourhood meets both conditions.

Worked example

If our focus is on areas that are darker than one-quarter of the mean intensity, we would choose k₀ = 0 and k₁ = 0.25. To enhance a dark area of low contrast, we might choose k₂ = 0 and k₃ = 0.1. Applying this selectively brightens only the qualifying dark, low-contrast regions (e.g. the interior of dark squares) while leaving the rest of the image untouched.
